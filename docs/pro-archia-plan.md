## Why this game exists: a tribute

In class, Prof. (Dr.) Prasannanshu set aside a line-by-line reading of
*Pro Archia* because he could see it was boring the room. That is exactly
Cicero's own move in the speech: at § 12 he stops arguing the technicalities
and speaks about why literature matters, because a jury that is not listening
cannot be persuaded. The game is built around that moment ("the turn", between Acts
III and IV), so a student who plays it does what both the orator and the teacher did:
notices the room, and changes course. The reflection page says so, as a
tribute to the professor.

## 1. The idea in one paragraph

Rome, 62 BC. An old Greek poet is about to be thrown out of the city because a
fire destroyed the papers that prove he is a citizen. The student is the young
**Marcus Tullius Cicero**, his former pupil, and has one morning, in the open
air of the Forum, to save him. A prosecutor argues back. Eight jurors react to
every word. A water clock runs out. Halfway through, the student wins the legal
argument and finds the jury has stopped listening; what they do next is the
same choice Cicero made, and the reason the speech is famous. At the end the
jury votes, and the student's speech is laid beside Cicero's real one.

**Aim**, as with the Kafka games: the student should not memorise the
structure and arguments of *Pro Archia*, but *build them under pressure*, and
understand why Cicero built them that way.

## 2. What the student learns

| Area | Learned by doing |
|---|---|
| **Law** | Citizenship under the *lex Plautia Papiria* (89 BC) and the *lex Papia* (65 BC); evidence when records are lost; testimony against documents; who has to prove what |
| **Rhetoric** | The five parts of a Roman speech (*exordium, narratio, confirmatio, refutatio, peroratio*); *ethos, pathos, logos*; knowing your audience; time as a resource |
| **Literature** | Cicero's defence of literature: why poetry matters to a person, a state and its glory; Homer, Ennius, Alexander at the tomb of Achilles |
| **Communication** | Answering an opponent, reading a room, choosing what to leave out |

## 3. The people (historical) and what is invented

**Historical:**

- **Aulus Licinius Archias.** Greek poet, born at Antioch about 120 BC; came to Rome in 102 aged about 17; client of the Luculli; made a citizen of Heraclea; Roman citizen under the law of 89 BC. Now about 60.
- **Marcus Tullius Cicero.** The player. 44 years old, consul the year before (63 BC), the most famous advocate in Rome, and Archias' pupil as a boy.
- **Quintus Tullius Cicero.** Cicero's younger brother, the praetor presiding over the court.
- **Grattius.** The prosecutor. Nothing is known of him except this trial; probably a supporter of Pompey, aiming at Lucullus through his poet.
- **Marcus Lucullus.** Witness: he took Archias to Heraclea and had him enrolled there.
- **The envoys of Heraclea.** Witnesses, with official written evidence.
- **Lucius Lucullus.** Archias' patron, the general of the Mithridatic War. Away from Rome.

**Invented (and labelled as such in the game's reflection page):**

- The **eight jurors** and their characters. Real juries at this date were drawn from three orders (senators, equestrians and *tribuni aerarii*), and we keep that mix.
- **Grattius' words.** His speech is lost; we rebuild his arguments from Cicero's replies, which is exactly how historians do it.
- **The night-before scene** with Archias.

## 4. How it plays: the systems

### 4.1 The jury (the heart of the game)

Eight jurors sit on two benches, each drawn as a figure who visibly reacts
(leans in, nods, folds his arms, yawns, whispers to his neighbour). Each has
three hidden values that the student learns to read from their faces:

- **Persuaded:** believes the law is on Archias' side (*logos*).
- **Well-disposed:** likes and trusts Archias and Cicero (*ethos, pathos*).
- **Attentive:** is still listening. This one falls on its own over time and with every long legal point.

| # | Juror | Order | Cares about | Bored or put off by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Sempronius**, old senator | senator | Greek learning, dignity, precedent | Cheap jokes, flattery |
| 2 | **Varro**, tax contractor | equestrian | Money, getting home for lunch | Long lists of names |
| 3 | **Titus Aufidius**, veteran of Marius' wars | *tribunus aerarius* | Glory, the army, Rome's victories | Greeks in general |
| 4 | **Atilius**, strict jurist | senator | Documents, procedure, exact law | Emotion instead of proof |
| 5 | **Publius Cornelius**, ambitious young noble | senator | Fame, being remembered | Old men's stories |
| 6 | **Gnaeus Pompeianus**, Pompey's man | equestrian | Pompey's honour | Praise of Lucullus |
| 7 | **Mamilius**, treasury official | *tribunus aerarius* | Practical facts, what the census says | Poetry |
| 8 | **Servius**, a lover of verse | equestrian | Poetry itself | Dry technicalities |

The verdict needs a **majority of the eight** (five votes). Each juror votes
from his three values: persuaded and well-disposed, he acquits; persuaded
but bored or hostile, he may still condemn out of spite or inattention.

### 4.2 The water clock (*clepsydra*)

Roman courts timed speeches with water clocks. Ours stands beside the
tribunal and empties as the student speaks. Every argument costs water;
long ones cost more. **The student cannot say everything**, and has to choose.
That is the real lesson of *narratio*: brief, clear, believable.

### 4.3 Grattius objects

At set moments Grattius stands and attacks. Play pauses, an **evidence table**
opens, and the student answers with one or two **evidence cards**. Answers
are strong, weak or harmful; Grattius uses weak answers against you
("So the defence *admits* there is no record!").

### 4.4 Evidence cards

The student collects cards in the prologue and by questioning witnesses.
**A fact never questioned for is a card never collected.**

| Card | Proves | Source in the speech |
|---|---|---|
| Testimony of Marcus Lucullus | Archias was enrolled at Heraclea: "not that he thinks it but knows it, not heard it but saw it, not was present but did it" | § 8 |
| The envoys of Heraclea | The town's official word and written evidence | § 8 |
| The record office fire | Why the Heraclean registers cannot be produced | § 8 |
| Years in Rome | A fixed residence in Italy long before 89 BC: home, property, the house of the Luculli | § 9 |
| The lists of Metellus | Declared before the praetor Metellus, whose lists were exact; he once reported a single erased name | § 9 |
| Appius and Gabinius | Other praetors' lists were careless and untrustworthy | § 9 |
| Other cities' citizenship | Tarentum, Rhegium, Locri and Neapolis also made him a citizen | §§ 5, 10 |
| On campaign | At the last census he was with Lucius Lucullus' army; at the one before, in Asia with Lucullus as quaestor | § 11 |
| The empty census | At the census of Julius and Crassus no one was registered at all | § 11 |
| Lived as a citizen | He made wills under Roman law, inherited from Roman citizens, and was recommended for a treasury reward by Lucullus | § 11 |

### 4.5 Questioning a witness

A short conversation. The student chooses questions; each answer either
reveals a card or wastes water. Good questions are specific ("Did you do it
yourself?"), poor ones are vague ("Is he a good man?").

### 4.6 Building the speech (Act IV)

The student is handed **argument cards** about literature. He chooses four,
then **puts them in order**. The jury hears them in that order. Some
arguments warm some jurors and cool others (praising Lucullus pleases most
and irritates Pompey's man; a joke wakes the tax contractor and offends the
old senator). Order matters: an emotional climax works at the end, not the
start.

### 4.7 The class as the jury (classroom board)

When played to a class, the game pauses at three moments and asks the room
a question ("Is the word of Lucullus enough without a document? Hands up").
The teacher taps the count (for example 18 of 30) and that sets the mood of
the swing jurors. On a laptop alone, these moments become the player's own
judgement. Offline, no accounts, no phones needed.

### 4.8 Score and endings

**Winning is never automatic.** The jury's mood is the sum of the student's
choices; a weak exordium, a muddled narrative, poor answers to Grattius or an
unordered defence of literature lose votes, and enough of them lose the case.
There is no "easy mode".


- **Acquitted with acclaim** (7 or 8 votes): the crowd cheers and Archias weeps.
- **Acquitted** (5 or 6 votes): a quiet relief.
- **Condemned** (4 or fewer): Archias is ordered to leave Rome, and we see him gather his scrolls. Then the game offers to try again.

Historically he was acquitted; losing is allowed so that the student feels
what the arguments were *for*.

## 5. The script

*Narration is in the second person, as in the Kafka games. Spoken lines are
voiced. ▸ marks a choice; → marks what it does.*

### Prologue: the night before (about 1 minute)

*Rome, 62 BC. Night. Cicero's house on the Palatine. Lamps, scrolls, a
writing table.*

> NARRATOR: You are Marcus Tullius Cicero. Last year you were consul and saved
> the Republic, or so you like to say. Tomorrow you defend an old man in
> court, and this case you cannot afford to lose.

*A knock. ARCHIAS enters: grey, thin, carrying a scroll case.*

> ARCHIAS: They say Grattius has the law of Papius in one hand and nothing in
> the other. But nothing is enough, Marcus, when the papers are ash.

*He sets down the case file. The student opens it. Three documents, read by
clicking:*

1. **The charge:** under the *lex Papia* (65 BC) a foreigner living in Rome without the citizenship must leave. Grattius says Archias is not a citizen.
2. **The law of 89 BC** (*lex Plautia Papiria*): citizens of allied towns who lived in Italy and declared themselves before a praetor within sixty days became Romans.
3. **A letter from Heraclea:** *"The record office burned in the war. We have nothing on paper. But we remember."*

> ▸ "You taught me everything, old friend. I will not let them send you away."
> → Starts *well-disposed* higher with Servius and Sempronius (they will hear the gratitude in your opening).
>
> ▸ "The law is clear. This will be quick."
> → Starts *persuaded* slightly higher; the turn in Act III will cost more.
>
> ▸ "Tell me everything, from Antioch onwards."
> → Archias tells his life (two minutes, voiced). You gain no card but **see** the timeline you will need in Act II. A careful player's reward.

*The title appears: **Save the Poet**, with its subtitle.*

*First insight unlocked: **"A debt."** Cicero opens his real speech by saying
that whatever skill he has, he owes to this man.*

### Act I: the Forum, morning (*exordium*, about 2 minutes)

*The Forum in sunlight: the Rostra, the Temple of Castor, a crowd. A raised
tribunal; QUINTUS, the praetor, in his chair; the jury on benches; ARCHIAS
sitting with his eyes down. The water clock is filled. You walk in through
the crowd (hold to walk, as in Before the Law).*

> QUINTUS: The court is in session. The charge is under the law of Gaius
> Papius. Grattius, you may speak.

*GRATTIUS stands. A short, sharp speech (about 40 seconds, voiced):*

> GRATTIUS: Judges, this man is a Syrian. A Greek who sings for his supper in
> the houses of the great. He says he is a citizen of Heraclea; show me
> Heraclea's register. He says he is a citizen of Rome; show me his name in
> one census, any census, in twenty-seven years. You cannot, because it is
> not there. The law of Papius was made for exactly this man. Send him home.

*Jurors react: Mamilius nods ("the census!"), Atilius writes something down,
Titus mutters about Greeks. The water clock is turned for you.*

**Your opening.** The student chooses how to begin:

> ▸ **Ask the court's indulgence.** Say that you will speak in an unusual way,
> about literature, because your client is a poet and this jury is learned.
> → Cicero's real choice (§§ 3–4). *Well-disposed* up for most jurors, a
> small loss with Atilius ("what has poetry to do with the law?"). **Unlocks**
> the full power of Act IV: you asked permission in advance.
>
> ▸ **Attack the prosecution as political:** Grattius is Pompey's errand boy.
> → Gnaeus Pompeianus turns hostile for the rest of the trial; two others
> enjoy it. Risky.
>
> ▸ **Go straight to the law.**
> → Neutral. Atilius approves. Act IV starts colder.

*Insight: **"Asking permission."** The *exordium* wins the audience before
the argument begins.*

### Act II: the story of a life (*narratio*, about 2 minutes)

*The view widens to a map of the Mediterranean in the air above the court.*

> NARRATOR: Tell them who he is. Briefly. The water is running.

The student is given **ten life cards** and lays them on a timeline. Only six
fit before the water runs low:

1. Born at Antioch, a rich and learned city
2. Famous across Asia and Greece as a young poet
3. Made a citizen by Tarentum, Rhegium and Neapolis
4. Arrives in Rome in 102 BC, still a boy, the year Marius and Catulus are consuls
5. Taken into the house of the Luculli
6. A friend of the Metelli, Catulus, Crassus, Drusus and the Hortensii
7. Goes to Sicily with Marcus Lucullus; enrolled as a citizen of Heraclea on the way home
8. 89 BC: declares himself before the praetor Metellus, within sixty days
9. Writes a poem on Marius' war against the Cimbri
10. Travels east with Lucius Lucullus in the Mithridatic War

→ Order matters: the **legal chain (4, 7, 8)** must be there and in order, or
Atilius and Mamilius lose the thread. Too many names (6) and Varro yawns.
Leaving out Antioch and fame (1, 2) costs warmth. A clean, brief, ordered
story earns the insight **"Brief, clear, believable."** That is the classical
rule for a *narratio*, and the student has just obeyed or broken it.

### Act III: proof (*confirmatio* and *refutatio*, about 3 minutes)

**First, a witness.** MARCUS LUCULLUS takes the stand.

> ▸ "Do you know Archias?" → wastes water ("All Rome knows him.").
> ▸ "Were you at Heraclea when he was enrolled?" → "I was."
> ▸ "Did you arrange it yourself?" → "I did it myself." **Card: Testimony of Marcus Lucullus.**
> ▸ "What happened to the town's records?" → "Burned, in the Italian war." **Card: The record office fire.**

The ENVOYS OF HERACLEA stand and present a sealed tablet. **Card: The envoys
of Heraclea.**

**Objection 1: "Where is Heraclea's register?"**

> GRATTIUS: A citizen of Heraclea? Then show the court Heraclea's register!

| Answer | Result |
|---|---|
| *The record office fire* alone | **Weak.** Grattius: "So there is no record. The defence admits it." Atilius cools |
| *Lucullus* + *the envoys* | **Strong.** |
| *Lucullus* + *the envoys*, then *the fire* | **Best.** Cicero's own line plays: *"It is absurd to say nothing of the proof we have, and demand the proof no one can have."* (§ 8). Insight: **"Memory against paper."** |

**Objection 2: "He never really lived in Rome."**
→ *Years in Rome* is strong. *Other cities' citizenship* is beside the point here (Grattius: "Then let Tarentum keep him!").

**Objection 3: "Even if he declared himself, those praetors' lists are worthless."**
→ *The lists of Metellus* + *Appius and Gabinius*: the contrast wins ("he worried over a single erased name; there is no erasure over Archias"). Mamilius, the treasury man, is won over. Insight: **"Not all records are equal."**

**Objection 4: "His name is in no census. Not one."**

> **The class-vote moment (1):** *"Is being missing from the census proof
> that a man is not a citizen? Hands up for no."*

→ Best: *On campaign* + *The empty census*, then *Lived as a citizen* ("the census only shows who claimed to be a citizen at the time; but he made wills under our laws..."). Insight: **"Absence is not evidence."**

*Optional:* *Other cities' citizenship* as a flourish at the end ("Towns that
gave citizenship to actors would refuse it to him?"). It warms Servius but
costs water.

> QUINTUS (dry): The court has heard the defence on the law. Have you more?

### Interlude: the turn (about 40 seconds)

*The legal case is won: the "persuaded" values are high. But look at the
benches. Varro is checking the sun. Publius is whispering. Titus' arms are
folded. The **attention** values have fallen, and you can see it.*

> GRATTIUS (sneering): Why such love for this little Greek, Cicero? What is a
> poet to a Roman court?

> **The class-vote moment (2):** *"Cicero has proved the law. Should he stop
> here? Hands up for stop."*

> ▸ **Rest your case.** You have proved the law.
> → The trial goes straight to the vote. Persuaded but bored jurors vote
> lazily; a narrow result at best. Insight: **"Proof is not persuasion."**
>
> ▸ **Pile on more legal points.**
> → Costs water, wins nothing, attention falls further.
>
> ▸ **Answer Grattius' real question: why does literature matter?**
> → Cicero's choice (§ 12). The jury looks up, the music shifts, and Act IV
> begins. Insight: **"Beyond the case."** This is the *extra causam*: the
> part of the speech outside the legal case, which makes up most of it.

### Act IV: the defence of literature (about 3 minutes)

*The light changes; the Forum seems to fill with the figures you speak of,
drawn in gold as the speech goes on: Homer, Ennius, Alexander, Achilles' tomb.*

The student chooses **four of eight argument cards** and orders them:

| Card | The argument (from the speech) | Warms | Cools |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rest for the mind** | Literature restores me after the din of the courts; I give it no more time than others give to dice and games (§§ 12–13) | Varro, Mamilius | (none) |
| **Through every age** | *"These studies nourish youth and delight old age..."* (§ 16) | everyone, a little | (none) |
| **Great men honoured poets** | Scipio Africanus kept Ennius at his side; Themistocles said the best singer was the one who best proclaimed *his* greatness (§§ 16, 20, 22) | Sempronius, Publius | (none) |
| **Alexander at Achilles' tomb** | *"Fortunate young man, to have found Homer as the herald of your courage!"* (§ 24) | Titus, Publius | (none) |
| **Poets are sacred** | Ennius calls poets sacred; rocks and deserts answer a poet's voice; seven cities fight over Homer, a foreigner, and shall Rome cast out a living poet who is hers by law? (§§ 18–19) | Servius, Sempronius | Atilius, a little |
| **Rome's own glory** | His poems on the Cimbrian and Mithridatic wars praise the Roman people; Greek carries our fame to every nation (§§ 19–23) | Titus, Publius, most | Gnaeus Pompeianus (Lucullus again!) |
| **Even Pompey did it** | Pompey made his historian Theophanes a citizen in front of his army (§ 24) | **Gnaeus Pompeianus**, Titus | (none) |
| **Sulla's bad poet** | Sulla paid a terrible poet a reward on condition he never write again (§ 25) | Varro (laughs), Mamilius | Sempronius |

Then **the peroration** (*peroratio*), one choice:

> ▸ **Law and humanity together** (Cicero's real ending, §§ 31–32): restate the
> four proofs (the law, the town, Lucullus, Metellus), then ask the jury to
> protect a man who has always honoured Rome, *"rescued by your humanity
> rather than injured by your severity."*
>
> ▸ **Only the law.** Safe and cool.
>
> ▸ **Only the heart.** Moving; Atilius and Mamilius may wander.
>
> ▸ **Mention your own consulship**: Archias has promised a poem about it (§ 28).
> → Vanity! Some jurors smile, Grattius smirks. A small lesson in *ethos*:
> Cicero really did this, and Romans laughed at him for it.

> **The class-vote moment (3):** the room votes on the verdict along with the jury.

### The verdict (about 1 minute)

*Each juror drops a wax tablet into the urn. Roman jurors marked **A**
(*absolvo*, I acquit) or **C** (*condemno*, I condemn). Quintus reads them out
one by one; the student watches each face.*

**Endings:** see 4.8. Whatever the result, the last voiced line is Archias':

> ARCHIAS (acquitted): You spoke for poetry, Marcus. Now poetry owes you a poem.
>
> ARCHIAS (condemned): Then I shall go where Greek is read. That is everywhere.

*(The second line paraphrases Cicero's own point in § 23 that Greek is read
almost everywhere.)*

### The reflection page

As in the Kafka games, built from what this student actually did:

1. **Your speech beside Cicero's.** Two columns: the order you chose, and Cicero's:
   - *exordium* (§§ 1–4): a debt, and a request to speak unusually
   - *narratio* (§§ 4–7): brief, ordered, the legal chain
   - *confirmatio* and *refutatio* (§§ 8–11): the four proofs
   - *extra causam* (§§ 12–30): the defence of literature
   - *peroratio* (§§ 31–32): law and humanity together
2. **What each juror thought of you**, in one line each.
3. **Key lines in Latin, with our translation:**
   - *Si quid est in me ingeni, iudices...* "If I have any talent, judges (and I know how little it is)..." (§ 1)
   - *Est ridiculum ad ea quae habemus nihil dicere, quaerere quae habere non possumus.* "It is absurd to say nothing of what we have, and demand what we cannot have." (§ 8)
   - *Haec studia adulescentiam alunt, senectutem oblectant, secundas res ornant, adversis perfugium ac solacium praebent.* "These studies nourish youth and delight old age; they adorn good fortune and give refuge and comfort in bad." (§ 16)
   - *O fortunate adulescens, qui tuae virtutis Homerum praeconem inveneris!* "Fortunate young man, to have found Homer as the herald of your courage!" (§ 24)
   - *Trahimur omnes studio laudis.* "We are all drawn on by the love of praise." (§ 26)
4. **Context:** Pompey against Lucullus; what happened to Archias (acquitted; no poem on Cicero's consulship survives).
5. **What is invented in this game**, listed honestly.
6. **Questions for discussion**, for example:
   - Grattius had the law's letter; Cicero had witnesses. When records are lost, what should count as proof?
   - Cicero won the legal case by § 11, then spoke for twenty more sections. Was that necessary, or showmanship?
   - Is it fair to persuade a jury with poetry when the question is legal? Where is the line in a modern court?
   - Cicero praises poets because they make great men famous. Is that a good reason to value literature? What other reasons does he give?
   - Who is Archias in today's world? Think of people whose papers were lost to war or disaster.

## 6. Look, sound and feel

- **Setting:** the Roman Forum on a spring morning in 62 BC. The trial is in the open air, as Roman criminal trials were: the praetor's raised tribunal, jurors' benches, a crowd standing behind, temples and the Rostra around. Warm stone, sunlight, a sky.
- **People:** togas (white; the praetor's with a purple border), Archias in Greek dress. The crowd stirs and murmurs and grows when Act IV begins, because a famous speech draws listeners.
- **Sound:** the Forum's crowd, a water clock dripping (faster as time runs short), a lyre motif for Archias that swells in Act IV, a single drumbeat for each verdict tablet.
- **Narration:** voiced like the Kafka games: narrator, Cicero (you, mostly unvoiced choices), Archias, Grattius, Quintus, Lucullus.
- **Latin toggle:** key lines can be heard or read in Latin.
- **Text-only version** and **classroom board mode**, as for the other games.

**Length:** about 13 to 15 minutes. A "short trial" option (no prologue, Act II shortened) brings it to about 8 for a class demonstration.

## 7. The text and copyright

The Oxford translation (D. H. Berry) is still in copyright, so the game
quotes none of it. Every English line in the game is written fresh from
Cicero's Latin, which is free to use, just as the Kafka games were written
from the German. Short Latin quotations appear with our own translations.
